A claim is not evidence

until it carries a receipt.

AI teams make promises: fast, private, accurate, battery-friendly, clean. This project turns each promise into a checkable receipt that anyone can verify, and that breaks visibly if anyone edits the story afterwards.

The idea

When you buy groceries, you get a receipt: what you bought, the price, the time, the shop. An elevator carries an inspection sticker: who checked it, against what standard, and when. AI systems make bigger promises than groceries and mostly hand you neither.

Our receipt holds five things: the promise, the bar it must clear, the number we actually saw, the verdict, and a cryptographic signature. Change any digit later and the signature breaks in public.

Verdicts work like traffic lights, and each one decides what happens next. PASS means continue. REVIEW means a named human must look before anything proceeds. BLOCK means stop. Nobody can wave a red light through, and the amber one can only be overridden by a person who signs their name, their reason, and its scope. That override becomes a receipt too.

Why this exists

14 / 15

MANOLO builds trustworthy, efficient AI across healthcare and industrial robotics, mobile computer vision, and wearable EEG. It already stores run metrics, tracks where data came from, and raises alerts while systems run. Its assessment method writes down every promise a system makes and marks which ones have cited evidence; in the one promise table published in full, the column titled "Evidence from Case Log" reads "no direct evidence cited" or "requires validation study" for fourteen of fifteen promises. The run logs exist. What is missing is the binding: nothing connects a specific promise to the specific run that would evidence it and keeps the verdict. We built that binding, and it is use-case-agnostic: the same co-design process ran for every use case, and the receipts in this repository already cover energy, cost, and benchmark claims that have nothing to do with any one domain. Two rows of that table are things a benchmark can simply measure: "the system answers in under 100 milliseconds" and "compressing the model keeps it accurate". We measured them on a comparable workload, wrote the verdicts as signed receipts, and gave the one promise that already had cited evidence its receipt as well. That instrument now exists here, open source, under Apache-2.0.

How it works

1. Measure. A benchmark runs the same task three ways and records speed, memory, and accuracy. One command, same result on any machine, because the test data is generated from a fixed seed.
2. Judge. Each promise is compared against the evidence. But first comes a question most systems skip: where did this number come from? We measured it, a named source published it, a vendor quoted it, or someone assumed it.
3. Receipt. The verdict is signed, chained to every verdict before it, and saved. Anyone can re-check the whole chain in under a second, forever.

When a good-looking number still fails

A number can look perfect and still not pass. In our demo, a cost estimate beats its target easily, but it is built on a guessed power draw. The system pauses it anyway: arithmetic on top of a guess is still a guess. And a vendor's quoted "82 milliseconds" pauses too, while a naive checker happily passes it. The gate refuses to turn guesses into green lights.

What this means in real life

A hospital buying a sleep wearable

The brochure says "real-time and private". Instead of trusting the brochure, procurement asks for receipts. A vendor quote alone shows as paused, never as passed. The evidence trail stays checkable after the meeting.

A funder or project officer

Research projects publish claim tables. Receipts make those tables checkable years later: who claimed what, against which evidence, and what the verdict was, with a signature that still verifies.

A team choosing where to run AI

The same workload, two countries, real public data. The cleaner and cheaper option gets the green light, and the decision itself becomes a signed record instead of an undocumented opinion.

Frequently asked questions

What is this in one sentence?

A tool that turns claims about an AI system, like "answers in under 100 milliseconds", into signed, checkable receipts, so decisions rest on records, not on whoever sounds most confident.

What exactly is a "claim"?

Any promise about an AI system stated with a number: "answers in under 100 milliseconds", "keeps 95 percent accuracy after compression", "runs on a grid cleaner than 100 grams of CO2 per kilowatt hour". If it has a bar to clear, it is a claim.

Is the receipt like a store receipt?

Very close. A store receipt records what happened in a purchase. Ours records what happened in a check: the promise, the bar, the observed number, the verdict, and when. The difference is the signature: edit ours afterwards and the forgery is detectable by anyone, in under a second.

Do I need to be a programmer to use it?

To read receipts, no: the demo page shows them as colored verdicts with plain reasons. To connect it to a new system, someone technical writes a small file listing the promises and their bars. That is the whole integration.

Can someone fake or quietly edit a receipt?

Edits are detectable, which is the honest promise. Every receipt is chained to the one before it and signed. Change any digit of the evidence or the receipt and verification fails, naming the exact spot. We demonstrate this live by breaking it on purpose.

Why do some of your own checks fail?

On purpose. We did not meter energy in joules, so our own energy claim pauses itself instead of pretending. A checking tool has to hold itself to the same bar it applies to everyone else.

What does the energy part mean for a normal company?

Where you run AI changes its carbon footprint and its electricity bill. The same workload can be nine times cleaner and almost half the price in one country versus another, using public data anyone can check. Receipts turn that choice into an accountable record.

Is this an official MANOLO tool?

No. It is an independent, open-source build for the MANOLO Innovation Lab, based on the project's public documents. It uses their vocabulary and is designed to plug in, and it never claims compatibility with systems that have no public interface yet.

Does it send my data anywhere?

No. Zero network calls, verified by the test suite. Everything runs and verifies locally, and the whole history replays from the repository.

What does it deliberately not do?

It does not meter energy in joules, does not store data lineage, does not replace live monitoring, and does not prove a model is safe or clinically effective. It proves one thing well: that a claim, its evidence, and its verdict are bound together and unedited.

What happens after the hackathon?

The code is Apache-2.0, so anyone can adopt or adapt it. The natural next step is pointing the contract at the project's own claims tables: the people who own each claim declare its bar, and every verdict arrives as a signed receipt.
